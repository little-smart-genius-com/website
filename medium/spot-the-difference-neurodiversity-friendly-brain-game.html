Every parent has been there. You print out a fun activity, slide it across the table, and watch your child’s face fall. The worksheet looks like chaos to them. Too much clutter, too many details, too many places for their eyes to land. That was my exact moment of realization with my own son. I assumed a classic spot the difference puzzle would be a quick win. Instead, he shut down before he even started.

Here’s what I learned: not every brain processes visual information the same way. And that’s exactly why the standard approach to these puzzles fails so many kids with ADHD, dyslexia, or autism. The good news? There’s a better way.

One mom I know, Sarah, told me her daughter Maya used to outright refuse these worksheets. The pages overwhelmed her. Too chaotic. Too many things competing for her attention at once. But once we made a few simple adjustments? Maya went from refusing to requesting the activity. The secret wasn’t changing the game. It was changing how we presented it.

What most people miss is that your child isn’t just “finding differences.” They’re actually building the same neural pathways used for reading. Spotting a missing stripe on a cat’s tail? That’s the exact comparison skill needed to tell the difference between a “b” and a “d.” Every scan, every pause, every squint is cross-coding between vision and cognition. This isn’t busywork. It’s brain training disguised as play.

So how do you actually use this without tears or frustration? Start by setting a relaxed vibe. This is not a test. The only rule I enforce? Keep a snack nearby and allow “twinkle breaks.” Sounds ridiculous, but it works. When my son feels his focus slipping, we stop, take three deep breaths, and look for something shiny in the room. It resets his brain completely.

Here are two actionable tips you can use today:

First, reduce visual clutter before starting. Cover half the page with a blank sheet of paper so your child only sees one section at a time. This single trick eliminated my son’s overwhelm completely.

Second, use a pointer instead of a finger. A chopstick, a pencil, or even a toothpick works. This physically guides their eyes and keeps them from getting lost on the page. For kids with dyslexia, this small change can make the difference between giving up and powering through.
