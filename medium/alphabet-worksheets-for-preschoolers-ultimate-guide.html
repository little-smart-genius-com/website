You’ve seen it happen. Your preschooler taps away on a tablet, matching letters to sounds, and you think: This is learning. This is productive. But here’s what the research quietly reveals—and what we parents rarely hear.

A 2019 study in the Journal of Experimental Child Psychology found that tracing letters on paper activates motor pathways in the brain that screens simply cannot reach. When your child’s hand moves a pencil across actual paper, their brain builds stronger neural connections for letter recognition. That app? It’s entertaining. But it’s not building the deep wiring your preschooler needs for reading and writing.

My friend Juliet learned this the hard way. Her five-year-old spent two weeks straight on a popular letter-building game. She was proud. Then she tested him: he couldn’t recognize the letter “M” outside the app. The game had taught him where to tap, not what the letter actually looked like. He had memorized a sequence, not a shape.

Here’s the good news: you can replace that screen time with something that actually sticks—and it doesn’t require fancy supplies or a teaching degree.

Start with our printable alphabet worksheets. They’re designed to do one thing: build real, lasting letter knowledge through touch and repetition. Here are two actionable tips from the full article that you can try today:

1. Trace and say aloud. Have your child trace each letter with their finger first, then with a crayon or pencil. As they trace, they say the letter’s name and sound. This dual-action—physical movement plus verbal repetition—cements learning far better than tapping a screen.

2. Use seasonal themes. The article explains how worksheets with pumpkins in October or snowflakes in January keep motivation high. Your child won’t even realize they’re practicing their ABCs because they’re busy coloring a Valentine’s heart or a summer beach ball. The variety prevents boredom and builds stamina across the year.
