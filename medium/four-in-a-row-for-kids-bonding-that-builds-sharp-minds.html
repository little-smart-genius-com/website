There’s a moment every parent knows: you pull out an activity, and your kid gives you the look. The skeptical side-eye that says, “Really? This again?” I got that look from my daughter Mara when she was six, the first time I introduced Four in a Row. She was unimpressed. Forty-five minutes later, my pancakes were cold, and I didn’t care. She was stretched across her chair, forehead scrunched in concentration, giggling every time she blocked my move. That’s the magic of this deceptively simple game. It doesn’t feel like learning. It feels like a showdown.

Here’s the thing about Four in a Row: it’s a bonding favorite because it levels the playing field. Kids don’t need to read or do math to play. They just need to see the board and think. And that’s exactly where the growth happens. Watching a child work through her first real game is special. Each round gets more thoughtful. The mistakes—the near-tears, the quick recoveries—they all build something. You’re not just connecting discs; you’re connecting neurons.

What is actually happening in that little brain? Pattern recognition, for starters. Kids start seeing sequences before they consciously think about them. They learn to anticipate your next move, which is a massive exercise in working memory and logic. But the sneaky win here is patience. Waiting for your turn, watching you deliberate, holding back that impulsive move—that’s executive function in action. For ages 4 to 12, this game is a stealth trainer for focus and emotional regulation.

So how do you play the “bonding way”? Grab a cuppa, print the sheet, and sit down with them. Don’t hand it off and disappear. Talk through your moves. Say things like, “Hmm, if I put it here, what do you think happens next?” This turns the game into a conversation rather than a competition. You want to model the thinking process out loud. It’s not about winning; it’s about showing them how to think about a problem.

Here are two actionable tips to try tonight:

- The "Think Aloud" Rule: Narrate your own strategy. “I’m going to block you here because I see you’re building a line.” This teaches strategy in a natural, non-lecturing way.

- The "Reverse POV" Trick: After a few rounds, ask your child, “If you were me, where would you put your disc?” This forces them to see the board from your perspective—a huge cognitive leap for younger kids.

The beauty is that the game adapts. For the little ones (ages 4-6), focus on matching colors and just getting three in a row to start. For the older kids (ages 8-12), introduce the creative variations—like playing with a "blocking only" rule or trying to make a square instead of a line. These twists keep the spark alive, ensuring the game never feels like a chore.

I print two versions of the sheet, just to keep things fresh. Sometimes we use big colored circles, sometimes we use shapes. The goal is to keep them engaged, not bored. Watching your child’s progress over time is the real reward. You’ll see the effort, the view of the board, the neat little strategies they develop on their own.
