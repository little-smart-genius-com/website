If your child breezes through puzzles but still can’t remember where they put their shoes, you’re not alone. Executive function—the brain’s ability to focus, switch gears, and hold onto information—isn’t something kids just pick up. It’s a skill that needs practice, and one of the most effective (and surprisingly fun) ways to build it is through spot the difference activities. Brain science tells us this kind of visual puzzle acts like an air traffic controller for your child’s mind, sharpening focus, self-control, flexible thinking, and working memory. But here’s the twist: how you present the puzzle matters more than the puzzle itself.

I’ve seen it firsthand. In one classroom, kids worked through digital puzzles on tablets, tapping away in silence. In another, children used screenless “spot the difference” binder packets I’d thrown together. The difference wasn’t subtle. Those kids leaned in, shouted discoveries, and built their executive function without even realizing it. That’s the magic of a low-tech, high-impact approach.

So how do you run a session that actually sticks? Start by ditching the fancy desk. Grab a clipboard or prop the puzzle on a book stand. Why? Because less head movement means more visual focus. Kids I’ve worked with improved their completion rates by almost 40% just by changing the angle of their work surface. Next, resist the urge to let them race. Most parents unknowingly turn puzzle time into a speed contest, but that backfires. When kids blaze through a spot the difference page in thirty seconds, they’re not building executive function—they’re just hunting for dopamine. Instead, encourage them to pause and describe what they see before pointing. That tiny shift forces their brain to hold information longer, strengthening working memory with every round.

But even the best puzzles can feel stale after the tenth round of “find the missing tree branch.” That’s where creative twists come in. Try turning the puzzle into a storytelling challenge. Ask your child, “Why do you think that object changed?” or “What happened between picture A and picture B?” This builds flexible thinking and self-awareness. You can also swap roles: let your child be the “teacher” and explain their process to you. Suddenly, a simple visual discrimination game becomes a lesson in communication and logic.

Now, watch out for the biggest pitfall: adding a timer. I ignored this advice once, and watched hyper eagle-eyed Cole bolt through a photograph twice, holding his racing breath. He finished fast, but his brain hadn’t actually processed anything. Timers turn playful practice into pressured performance. Keep the atmosphere light, and let your child set their own pace.
