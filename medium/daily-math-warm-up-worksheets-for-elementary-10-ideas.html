You know that August feeling. The scent of fresh crayons, the crispness of unopened notebooks, the quiet hope that this school year might be different. Then, a week in, your child stares at their math workbook like it’s written in a foreign language. The dreaded summer slide isn’t just a myth—it’s that blank stare over breakfast, the frustration before the school day even begins. But here’s what I’ve learned the hard way: that slide stops cold with one simple shift. You don’t need a full curriculum overhaul. You need a warm-up.

Think of it like stretching before a run. You wouldn’t ask a kid to sprint cold, right? Yet we often expect them to jump straight into complex problems without getting their brain moving first. A daily math warm-up is that five-minute window where the pressure is off. It’s not about getting the right answer. It’s about getting the brain to remember that math can be playful. The best part? It doesn’t require a degree in education or a stack of expensive apps. It just requires a tiny slice of your morning and a shift in perspective.

Here’s the trick most people miss: we assume our kids already know how to count. But counting with purpose is where the real magic lives. Instead of just reciting numbers, try a quick activity where they count objects around the kitchen—spoons, crackers, steps to the door. This builds number sense without the worksheet dread. Another game-changer? Make sure the warm-up sparks a little joy. If the activity makes your child groan, you’re doing it backwards. A warm-up should wake up the brain, not put it to sleep before breakfast. Keep it fast, keep it silly, and keep it low-stakes.

The secret sauce, though, is routine. Not a rigid schedule, but a predictable rhythm. When kids know what to expect—a quick counting game, a pattern puzzle, or a mental math riddle—they settle faster. Their brains flip a switch from "relax mode" to "ready mode." I’ve seen it work in my own kitchen. A consistent five-minute warm-up before the heavy work changes the entire mood of the morning. No more tears over place value. No more arguing about "why we have to do this."
