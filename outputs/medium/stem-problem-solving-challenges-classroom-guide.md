# STEM Problem Solving Challenges: Classroom Guide

*By Rachel Nguyen · Little Smart Genius*

---

![Featured Image](https://littlesmartgenius.com/images/og/stem-problem-solving-challenges-classroom-guide.jpg)

You know that feeling when you walk into a classroom supply closet and wonder if you need another expensive STEM kit? I’ve been there too. But here’s the truth I’ve learned after a decade of teaching: the best STEM problem solving challenges start with **simple materials you already have**. A pack of paper clips. A cup of rice. A roll of tape. That’s it.

Why does this work so well? Because when you strip away the flashy toys, you hand the problem back to the child. A fancy robot kit often defines the problem for them—so where’s the real experimentation? But give a kindergartner a handful of sand and some straws, and suddenly they’re building structures you never imagined. The magic isn’t in the materials. It’s in the **open-ended challenge** that asks them to figure things out on their own.

For the K–2 crowd (ages 4 to 7), I’ve narrowed it down to three non-negotiable challenges. The marble run built from crooked tubes? Pure gold. The look on a five-year-old’s face when her marble finally rolls through is the kind of moment that reminds me why I teach. These activities aren’t just about building—they’re about **building confidence**. And the best part? You don’t need a single worksheet to start. Just a challenge, a timer, and a willingness to let them get messy.

But here’s where most classroom guides stop. They end when the tower is built. I’d argue that’s when the real learning begins. After the timer goes off, I pull out simple journals—just a piece of paper folded in half. The first time I tried this, I expected kids to want to dash off to the next activity. Instead, they surprised me. They wanted to draw their designs, write about what went wrong, and share their “aha” moments. The post-challenge reflection turned a fun activity into a **lasting lesson** in critical thinking.

One actionable tip I swear by: Don’t skip the “mess-up” conversation. Ask your child or student, “What was the hardest part?” and “What would you change next time?” Those two questions unlock more cognitive growth than a dozen perfect builds.
