# How to Help Anxious Kids with Calming Coloring Routines

*By David Moreau · Little Smart Genius*

---

![Featured Image](https://littlesmartgenius.com/images/og/how-to-help-anxious-kids-with-calming-coloring-routines.jpg)

Connection takes practice—and crayons. It sounds a bit corny at first, I get it. But after a decade-plus working with kids, I’ve watched something magical happen when little fingers pick up those thick wax sticks. Coloring isn’t just about staying inside the lines—it’s about slowing down together. When my niece—she’s 4—and I started sitting together after her preschool pickup, something weird happened. Around day four, she stopped focusing on “getting done.” She leaned into the moment, and so did I. That’s where the real connection begins.

Most parents expect their kid to sit at the kitchen table with the TV blaring and wonder why it doesn’t calm anyone down. But when my daughter—she was just 6 at the time—started coloring in a quiet corner we set up together, everything shifted. Find a quiet nook away from traffic flow, away from screens and noise. Put up a plant or two—real ones if you can keep them alive, fake if you can’t. The goal isn’t perfection; it’s presence. That little space becomes a safe harbor for anxious minds.

Here’s the part that surprises most parents: coloring builds brains. I know that sounds dramatic, but think about it for a second. When your child quietly fills that giraffe’s spotted pattern blue instead of brown, are they testing independence? Gathering trust proof? Probably just exploring, but the fine motor work happening in those tiny hands is real. The grip strength, the hand-eye coordination, the decision-making—it all comes together naturally when they’re lost in color. And creativity? That blooms when there’s no pressure to “get it right.”

Now, what about the reluctant colorer? The kid who pushes the crayons away and says it’s boring? Start small. Let them choose the page—even if it’s just a simple shape. Use a single crayon at first. No rules about staying inside the lines. Sometimes the resistance isn’t about coloring itself; it’s about the fear of not being good enough. Take the pressure off by coloring alongside them without any agenda. Let them see you make “mistakes” and laugh about it.

Want to try this tonight? Grab a few simple printable pages from Little Smart Genius, pick one crayon each, and sit side by side for just five minutes. No talking about the day, no questions. Just color. You might be surprised at what comes up when the quiet settles in.

The full article walks you through setting up that calming corner, overcoming resistance step by step, and understanding exactly how these routines boost both fine motor skills and emotional connection. It’s packed with practical tips you can use tonight—no fancy supplies or expert training required. Because sometimes the simplest tools—a crayon, a page, a few quiet minutes—are exactly what your anxious child needs to feel safe and seen.
