# How Hitori Puzzles Helped Little Leo Conquer Math Anxiety

*By Little Smart Genius · Little Smart Genius*

---

![Featured Image](https://littlesmartgenius.com/images/og/how-hitori-puzzles-helped-little-leo-conquer-math-anxiety.jpg)

Remember that moment when your child just *shuts down* over a math problem? The pencil drops, the shoulders slump, and suddenly you're both stuck in a loop of frustration that helps no one. That was us with Leo, our seven-year-old. Math homework had become a daily battle zone, and I was running out of ideas that didn't involve tears or bribery.

Then I stumbled onto something completely unexpected: a simple grid puzzle called Hitori. I didn't tell Leo it was "educational." I just called it "the secret number detective game" and slid a tiny grid across the table. The rules are deceptively simple — cross out numbers so no digit repeats in any row or column, but you can't have shaded squares touching. That's it. No equations, no word problems, no pressure. Just a quiet puzzle that feels more like a game than work.

Here's what surprised me most: Leo didn't suddenly become a math genius overnight. But after he solved his first tricky Hitori grid, something shifted in his posture. A quiet sense of inner win. He started approaching math differently — not with dread, but with curiosity. These puzzles build something deeper than number skills. They teach **patience, careful observation, and the ability to hold multiple possibilities in your mind** without panicking. That's exactly what math anxiety crushes, and exactly what Hitori gently rebuilds.

If you want to try this at home, here's the approach that worked for us. First, **pick a quiet 15-minute slot** with no timers, no grading. Just a soft pencil and the grid. Second, **play alongside your child** — solve your own puzzle while they solve theirs. Kids learn best when they see us engaging, not just instructing. And third, **start absurdly small** — like a 4x4 grid for younger kids — so the first win comes fast and feels amazing.

The best part? Hitori adapts beautifully for ages 4 to 12. For little ones, use larger grids with fewer numbers and colorful markers instead of shading. For older kids, introduce 8x8 puzzles or even timed challenges where they race against their own best score. When interest starts to fade — and it will — get creative. Try "reverse Hitori" where you fill in the shaded squares first, or turn it into a two-player race on separate grids.

If your child has ever looked at a math worksheet like it was a personal enemy, I promise this is worth your time. **One tiny grid game completely changed how Leo sees numbers** — and it can do the same for your family.
