# Tic Tac Toe for Reluctant Learners: Sparks Critical Thinking

*By David Moreau · Little Smart Genius*

---

![Featured Image](https://littlesmartgenius.com/images/og/tic-tac-toe-for-reluctant-learners-sparks-critical-thinking.jpg)

I remember those days all too well. You hand your child a worksheet, and it’s like you’ve asked them to solve a Rubik’s Cube blindfolded. The pushback. The tears. The sudden fascination with the dust bunnies under the couch. If that sounds familiar, you’re not alone. I’ve been there as a teacher and as a mom. My own son, fiercely independent and stubborn as a mule, refused anything that smelled like “learning” for months. Then I tried something almost laughably simple: **tic tac toe.**

And honestly? It felt like cheating.

This classic game isn’t just a time-filler for fast finishers. I used to think that too. But watching a reluctant learner actually *start thinking* changed my mind completely. Tic tac toe is stealth skills development disguised as play. It builds **patience** when they lose. It teaches them to **adjust after mistakes** and replay to improve. They start **planning ahead** without realizing they’re doing it. No boring lectures, just the quiet hum of a brain working hard.

Here’s the kicker: you already know the rules. X goes first, O follows, three in a row wins. Simple, right? But if you approach it without thought, you’ll lose their attention fast. My son — nine years old, hyper-focused on everything except what I want him to learn — was crumbling playdough under the table while I sped through nine grids. Defeat in twenty seconds flat. He thought it was hilarious. Then he beat me.

That moment taught me something crucial: **the way you play matters more than the game itself.** Instead of rushing, slow down. Talk through your moves out loud. Say things like, “Hmm, if I put my X here, what will you do next?” This invites them into the logic without pressure. It turns a quick round into a conversation about strategy.

Another tip that worked wonders: **play backwards.** Start with a nearly full board and have them figure out who wins next. It flips the problem-solving upside down and keeps their brain engaged without the frustration of starting from scratch. And for the truly resistant ones, let them win. Not every time — but enough to build confidence. Once they taste victory, they’ll want to play again. And again. And that’s when the real learning happens.
