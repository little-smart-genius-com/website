# 5 Biggest Mistakes in Spot the Difference (Buildings) Fixes

*By Little Smart Genius · Little Smart Genius*

---

![Featured Image](https://littlesmartgenius.com/images/og/5-biggest-mistakes-in-spot-the-difference-buildings-fixes.jpg)

You know that moment when your child grabs a spot-the-difference puzzle, points at the screen with pure joy, and then tosses it aside forever? I’ve been there, and I bet you have too. Most of us treat these puzzles like a finish line—find the differences, celebrate, move on. But here’s the truth: that approach is actually shortchanging your child’s visual development.

The biggest mistake? Treating spot-the-difference like a one-and-done game. When kids race through a puzzle and never return to it, they miss out on a powerful training ground for observation skills. I saw this firsthand with six-year-old Maya. She grabbed the same spot-the-difference buildings puzzle three times in one week. On round one, she spotted three differences. By round three? She found all eight. The magic wasn’t in the puzzle itself—it was in the **repetition** that trained her brain to scan more carefully.

Another common slip-up is not scaffolding the difficulty. We’ve all grabbed a puzzle that looked fun, only to watch our child breeze through it in under a minute—or melt down because it was way too hard. The sweet spot is a gentle climb: start with simple, high-contrast differences, then gradually introduce more subtle ones. Think of it like building blocks for the eyes.

Here’s a practical tip you can use today: **teach the process, not just the answer.** When your child finds a difference, don’t just say “You got it!” and move on. Ask them *how* they spotted it. Did they notice the missing chimney? The different window color? This conscious scanning turns a casual find into a lasting skill. The difference they “found” casually often fades by dinnertime, but the scanning technique sticks.

And please, resist the urge to focus only on speed. I watched a second grader tear through an entire booklet of simple puzzles in minutes. He loved it, but he wasn’t actually learning to observe—he was just racing. Instead, encourage your child to slow down, compare left to right systematically, and verbalize what they see. That process builds a foundation for real visual perception.

Finally, don’t ignore **visual approach transfer**. The skills your child builds with buildings puzzles—comparing shapes, noticing missing details, scanning systematically—apply directly to reading, math, and even science. When they learn to spot a missing chimney in a snowy cottage scene, they’re also learning to spot a missing letter in a word.
