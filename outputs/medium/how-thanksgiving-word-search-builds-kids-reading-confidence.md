# How Thanksgiving Word Search Builds Kids' Reading Confidence

*By Sarah Mitchell · Little Smart Genius*

---

![Featured Image](https://littlesmartgenius.com/images/og/how-thanksgiving-word-search-builds-kids-reading-confidence.jpg)

You know that stack of Thanksgiving word searches you printed out? The one your kid glanced at for exactly thirty seconds before wandering off? I’ve been there, too. But here’s the thing—those little puzzles can actually *build reading confidence* if you use them the right way. Not the “busy work” way. The real way.

Let me show you what I mean.

The biggest mistake parents make is handing over a word search with zero setup. You just put it on the table and hope for the best. But a child who feels unsure about reading will look at that grid of letters and freeze. They don’t know where to start. So instead of building confidence, you’ve accidentally created a wall. Here’s the fix: **start with just three words.** Point to the first one. Say it out loud together. Then show them how to scan left to right, just like reading a sentence. That simple shift turns a puzzle into a reading lesson that doesn’t feel like one.

Now let’s talk about the fun part—**the tools that make kids feel like secret agents.** Grab a highlighter. Or better yet, a bingo dabber. I watched a second grader named Violet nearly pop with excitement when I handed her a neon pink marker just for word finding. That’s not a gimmick. That’s a confidence booster. When kids get to use a “special” tool, the task becomes a game. They stop worrying about getting it wrong and start focusing on the hunt. The result? They practice letter recognition, spelling patterns, and vocabulary without even realizing it.

Here’s another trick that works like magic: **turn word searches into spelling bee practice at home.** After your child finds a word, ask them to spell it aloud. Then cover it up and have them write it on a separate piece of paper. This builds the bridge between seeing a word and owning it. For younger kids, ages 4 to 6, stick to puzzles with large fonts and only three or four words, all horizontal. No diagonal or backward words yet. That keeps frustration low and success high. Every completed puzzle is a small victory that says, “I can do this.”

And that feeling? It carries over into real reading. When a child learns to scan for letters in a word search, they’re practicing the same skill they need to track words on a page. It’s low-pressure repetition that builds fluency without the pressure of a book report.

You don’t need fancy programs or extra screen time. You just need the right puzzle and a few intentional minutes.
