# 6-Week Counting Celebration: Track Number Sense Growth

*By Sarah Mitchell · Little Smart Genius*

---

![Featured Image](https://littlesmartgenius.com/images/og/6-week-counting-celebration-track-number-sense-growth.jpg)

You’re watching your child count—1, 2, 3, 4, 5—and they’re grinning, proud of themselves. But then you ask, “How many blocks are there?” and they just stare. Sound familiar? That moment when you realize they’ve memorized the words but haven’t connected them to actual quantities. It’s not a failure on their part—or yours. It’s a sign they’re building something deeper.

**Here’s the thing: counting struggles are actually a good sign.** When a child stumbles over numbers, it means their brain is working to understand *why* those words matter. They’re not just parroting a song. They’re wrestling with the concept of value, order, and quantity. That’s real number sense growth in action. And if you’ve ever worried your child is “behind,” take a breath. This is exactly where the magic happens.

So how do you turn those fumbles into confident counting? With a simple, six-week plan that feels more like play than practice. Start by using a printable counting numbers activity that pairs each numeral with a visual—like dots, animals, or stars. The first week, just let your child explore. Point to each item as you count together. Week two, ask them to touch each object while they say the number. Week three, mix it up: put five items in a row, then ask, “How many if I take one away?” You’ll see the lightbulb flicker.

**The secret sauce is tracking progress.** Use a simple chart or sticker sheet to mark each small win—counting to ten without help, recognizing the number 7, or correctly guessing how many crackers are on a plate. Those little celebrations build momentum. By week four, your child won’t just count. They’ll start estimating, comparing, and even noticing patterns. That’s when you know number sense has clicked.

To keep the energy high, try creative variations. Count steps as you walk to the car. Count the number of red cars you see on a drive. Or turn snack time into a game: “Can you give me four grapes?” These real-world connections make numbers feel useful, not abstract. And every time your child succeeds, mark it on your tracker. They’ll start asking to practice.

**Here’s the truth: this isn’t about perfection.** It’s about building a foundation that lasts. By the end of six weeks, your child won’t just recite numbers. They’ll own them—understanding that “five” means five fingers, five blocks, or five bites of dinner. And you’ll have a record of every step they took to get there.
